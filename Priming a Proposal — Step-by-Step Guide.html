Before investing a single hour in a proposal, you need to answer this question honestly. Most firms lose not because their proposal was bad — but because they never should have submitted in the first place. A rigorous Go/No-Go saves you from your own optimism.

The Five Questions to Answer Before You Write Anything

✅
1. Do We Have the Experience?
  • Have we done this type of project before?
  • Do we have 2–3 directly comparable projects to show?
  • Are those projects completed — not just in progress?
  • Do we have measurable outcomes to cite (LEED credits, budget adherence, client satisfaction)?
🤝
2. Do We Have a Relationship?
  • Have we spoken with someone inside this organization before?
  • Do they know our firm's name before this RFP dropped?
  • Do we know the decision-makers, or at least the prime architect?
  • Have we had any interaction — LinkedIn, event, referral — in the past 12 months?
⏱️
3. Do We Have the Capacity?
  • Is the proposal deadline realistic given current workload?
  • If we win, can we deliver on time and within budget?
  • Do we have the right subconsultants ready to commit?
  • Can we absorb this project without burning out the team?
💰
4. Is the Fee Realistic?
  • Does the estimated fee cover our fully-burdened costs?
  • Is there margin for scope creep and revisions?
  • Is the client known for paying on time?
  • Does winning this contract advance our strategic goals — even if the margin is thin?
🎯
5. Can We Write a Compelling Win Statement?
The test: Can you complete this sentence in one paragraph — without being vague? If you can't, you probably shouldn't submit.
"We should win this because we are [the only / one of the few] [firm type] with [specific credential] — close enough to [project location] to serve as a true local partner, fluent enough in [client language] to deliver their goals without a learning curve, and present enough in [client's community] to already know the [decision-makers / architects / engineers] assembling this team."
If you can fill in the brackets with specific, verifiable facts — go. If you're reaching — stop. Submit only when you can win. Every failed proposal is an investment with zero return.
The Hard Truth About Proposal Win Rates
The average LA firm wins 20–30% of proposals submitted. The best firms win 50–70% — because they are ruthless about Go/No-Go. They submit fewer, win more.
A proposal takes 40–120 hours of billable-equivalent time. At $150/hr, that's $6,000–$18,000 in unbillable effort per submission. Choose carefully.
The firms that win most consistently had a conversation with the client before the RFP dropped. If you're reading the RFP for the first time on release day, you're already behind the firm that's going to win.
Step 1 of 6
Know Your Client
Every client trusts technical competence as a baseline. What separates winning proposals is understanding what each client type values beyond competence — and writing directly to that value. Generic proposals lose. Client-specific proposals win.

The Trust Framework — What Every Client Needs First

Trust — The Universal Baseline
All clients · Technical competence · Regulatory compliance · Risk reduction
Then, what else? This is where your proposal either connects or gets cut.
🏗️ Developers
Private developers · Mixed-use · Residential · Transit-Oriented Development
Yield and ROI
Frame your design as a revenue generator, not a cost center. Show how landscape increases marketable units, reduces tenant turnover, supports higher rents, or accelerates lease-up. They are not buying landscape — they are buying yield.
🏛️ Architects / Contractors / Public Agencies
Municipalities · Institutions · AEC prime consultants
Schedule, budget, deliverables
They need to know you won't be the bottleneck. Emphasize your project management systems, subconsultant coordination experience, and track record of on-time deliverables. Reliability is your differentiator.
🏢 Public Companies
Corporate campuses · Tech · Healthcare · Hospitality
ESG and wellness goals
They are reporting to investors and boards. LEED credits, biophilic design, stormwater performance, and employee wellness outcomes are not nice-to-haves — they are investor-facing metrics. Speak their language: carbon sequestration, heat island reduction, Fitwel credits.
🏡 Homeowners
Residential · Private estates · High-net-worth clients
Lifestyle and long-term value
They are buying a vision of how they will live. Show them the experience, not the plan. Reference comparable projects where you transformed how owners use their outdoor space. Long-term maintenance, plant longevity, and seasonal interest matter here more than anywhere else.

How to Research Your Client Before Writing a Single Word

🔍
Public Clients
  • · Read the full RFP — every word, every exhibit
  • · Review their General Plan, Climate Action Plan, and prior project EIRs
  • · Attend the pre-proposal conference — always
  • · Submit written questions — your questions signal sophistication
  • · Review their council/board meeting minutes for project history
🏢
Corporate / Developer
  • · Read their annual report and ESG/CR report
  • · Review their 10-K for stated sustainability goals
  • · Study their LinkedIn presence — what do they post about?
  • · Research their other campuses — what LA firms did they use?
  • · Know their LEED and Fitwel certification targets by name
🎯
All Clients
  • · Use their exact language — mirror their vocabulary back
  • · Name their project by name in every relevant section
  • · Never submit a generic cover letter
  • · Reference what they said they wanted — then show you deliver it
  • · Know the decision-maker's name, title, and professional background

Key Milestones — Build Your Version Around These

Non-Negotiable Deadlines
  • Day 1–2Read every word of the RFP. Highlight mandatory requirements.
  • Day 5–7Go/No-Go decision made. Don't wait — scope and team definition depends on it.
  • Day 16Subconsultant materials due to you — not the day before due date.
  • Day 19Full draft complete. Internal review. No more writing.
  • Day 27+File prep, printing, delivery. Buffer for anything unexpected.
Common Scheduling Mistakes
Waiting until day 10 to make Go/No-Go — by then you've already invested unbillable hours and it's hard to say no.
Giving subconsultants the same deadline as your due date — their delay becomes your problem.
Treating the due date as the "finish" date — your final review, file prep, and delivery need 3–5 days after writing is done.
Not attending the pre-proposal conference — this is where you learn what the client actually cares about, not just what the RFP says.

What Makes Each Section Win — The Inside View

✉️
1. Letter of Introduction
  • · Never generic — name the project, the client, the specific challenge
  • · Lead with your win statement — why you, why now, why this project
  • · Signed by the principal who will actually lead the project
  • · One page maximum — evaluators read hundreds of these
📁
3. Firm Experience — The Most Important Section
  • · Lead with your most comparable project — not your most impressive
  • · Every project sheet must have: scope, budget, your role, client contact, measurable outcome
  • · "Similar in size, scope, and complexity" — use this language, it mirrors RFP evaluation criteria
  • · Include at least one project where something went wrong and you solved it — shows maturity
🧠
7. Approach and Work Plan — Where You Win or Lose
  • · Show you understand their specific challenges — not just the general project type
  • · Reference their stated goals using their exact language from the RFP
  • · Include a risk/mitigation section — shows you've thought past the optimistic scenario
  • · Be specific about deliverables — "we will provide" not "we will work to provide"
💲
9. Project Fee — Price to Win, Not to Low-Ball
  • · Know the client's budget range before you price — ask at pre-proposal if not stated
  • · Price to deliver at the quality level you've promised — low fees signal you'll cut corners
  • · Break out hours by phase and task — it shows rigor and builds confidence
  • · Include a reimbursable expenses allowance — never absorb these costs into your base fee
Step 4 of 5
Submit & Win
The proposal is written. Now the work is execution, quality control, and making sure you don't lose on a technicality after all the effort you've invested. Then — whether you win or lose — the follow-through defines your next opportunity.

Pre-Submission Checklist — Complete Every Item

📋
Content Compliance
  • Every required section included in correct order
  • Page limits respected — counted and verified
  • Font size and margin requirements met
  • All addenda acknowledged and included
  • All required forms completed (DBE, insurance, certifications)
  • References contacted and confirmed available
✏️
Quality Control
  • Spell-check completed — including proper nouns and project names
  • Client name spelled correctly throughout — every instance
  • Project name used consistently — no "find/replace" errors
  • All fees add up correctly — math verified by a second person
  • Photos and graphics are high resolution — minimum 150 dpi
  • Someone outside the project team has read the full document
📦
Submission Requirements
  • Correct number of copies (hard copy and/or digital)
  • Correct file format (PDF naming convention per RFP)
  • Submission method confirmed (portal, email, hand delivery)
  • Delivery address and room number verified
  • Submission deadline time confirmed — not just date
  • Confirmation receipt obtained / delivery tracked
📞
After Submission
  • Send a brief confirmation to the contact — acknowledge receipt
  • Note the interview date in your calendar if interviews are part of the process
  • Prepare for the interview immediately — don't wait for the shortlist notification
  • Request a debrief regardless of outcome — win or lose, learn from it
  • Log the pursuit in your BD tracker with hours invested and outcome

If You Don't Win — The Follow-Through That Sets Up the Next Win

What to Do When You Lose
Request a formal debrief immediately. Most public agencies are required to provide one. Private clients often will if asked respectfully. This is the single most valuable source of competitive intelligence available to you.
Ask specifically: what was the scoring breakdown? Which section were we weakest in? What did the winning firm do better? Take notes — these answers directly improve your next submission.
Thank the selection committee contact. Express genuine interest in the next opportunity. The relationship you build in defeat often leads to the win on the next RFP — especially with public agencies that issue work regularly.
Track your win rate by client type, project type, and project scale. If you're winning 30% of parks projects but 60% of campus projects — that data tells you where to focus your pursuit energy.
The Compounding Return: Every proposal you submit — win or lose — builds your firm's proposal infrastructure. Your project sheets get sharper. Your win statement gets tighter. Your team gets faster. The first proposal takes 120 hours. The tenth takes 60. The thirtieth takes 30. This is the investment that pays forward.
